Borrowing

How Loan Amortization Works, Step by Step

By the Calculate Clearly Editorial Team · Educational guide · Last reviewed October 2026

When you borrow money and repay it in equal monthly instalments, you are using an amortized loan. Car loans, mortgages, and most personal loans work this way. The payment stays the same, but what the payment does changes every month.

The one idea underneath everything

Interest is charged on the balance you still owe. At the start the balance is large, so a large share of your payment is interest and only a small share reduces the loan. As the balance falls, interest takes a smaller share and more of the same payment attacks the principal. Nothing about your payment changed — the mix inside it did.

A worked example

Suppose you borrow $20,000 for 5 years at 7% annual interest. The monthly payment is about $396.

MonthPaymentInterestPrincipalBalance after
1$396$117$279$19,721
12$396$108$288$16,493
36$396$76$320$10,542
60$396$2$394$0

Total paid is roughly $23,761, so the loan costs about $3,761 in interest — even though the rate sounded modest.

Reading an amortization schedule

What each column tells you

Principal is the part that actually shrinks your debt. Interest is the lender's charge for that month. The balance column is the most useful one: it tells you what you would owe if you sold the car, refinanced, or paid the loan off early on that date.

Why the payoff amount is not “payments left × payment”

Future payments contain future interest you have not incurred yet. Your payoff is the current balance plus a few days of interest — usually much less than the remaining payments added together.

How extra payments change the picture

An extra $50 a month on the example above, applied to principal, shortens the loan by several months and saves several hundred dollars in interest. The saving comes from lowering the balance earlier, when interest is most expensive. Before doing this, check two things: whether your loan has a prepayment penalty (uncommon but possible), and whether the lender applies extra money to principal rather than simply advancing your due date — you may need to specify that.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing loans by monthly payment alone, ignoring the term — a longer term lowers the payment but raises total interest.
  • Assuming half the loan is repaid halfway through the term. With amortization you have repaid less than half the balance at the midpoint.
  • Forgetting fees. The interest rate and the APR are different when fees are financed into the loan.

Key takeaway

Amortization is front-loaded interest and back-loaded principal. Once you understand that single pattern, payoff quotes, extra payments, and refinancing offers all become much easier to judge.
