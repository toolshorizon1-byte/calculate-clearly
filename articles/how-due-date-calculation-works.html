How Pregnancy Due Dates Are Calculated (And Why Babies Ignore Them)

By the Calculate Clearly Editorial Team · Educational guide · Last reviewed October 2026

A due date looks precise — one specific day, circled on the calendar. In reality it is the centre of a several-week window, produced by a rule from the 1800s that still anchors modern practice. Understanding the rule explains both the date and its uncertainty.

Naegele's rule

The standard calculation, named after obstetrician Franz Naegele, is:

  1. Take the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP).
  2. Add 7 days.
  3. Subtract 3 months.
  4. Add 1 year.

Equivalently: due date = LMP + 280 days (40 weeks).

Example: if the LMP began on 10 January, add 7 days (17 January), subtract 3 months (17 October of the previous year), add a year — the due date is 17 October.

The assumption hidden inside the rule

The 280 days are counted from the LMP, but conception usually happens about 14 days later, at ovulation — so the actual pregnancy length assumed is about 266 days. The rule also assumes a textbook 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14.

Why your date may be adjusted

  • Longer or shorter cycles: with a 35-day cycle, ovulation likely happened around day 21, pushing the realistic due date about a week later. Good calculators ask about cycle length for exactly this reason.
  • Uncertain LMP: if the date is unknown or periods are irregular, an early ultrasound measurement is considered more reliable and is commonly used to set or revise the date.
  • Known conception date (for example with fertility treatment): counting 266 days from conception is more direct.

Why babies ignore the circled day

Only a small minority of babies — around 4–5% in commonly cited statistics — are born on their exact due date. “Full term” spans weeks, not a day: births from 37 to 42 weeks fall within the normal range, with most babies arriving in the week either side of the estimate. The due date is best treated as a planning midpoint: useful for scheduling leave, preparing a nursery, and timing medical checks — not as an appointment the baby has agreed to.
